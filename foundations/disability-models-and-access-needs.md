# Disability models and access needs explained

## Models explain disability; descriptors describe experience

Medical and social models are perspectives on disability, not two mutually exclusive demographic groups. Apparent, non-apparent, temporary, fluctuating, age-related, situational and environmental describe different dimensions and can coexist.

| Perspective | Main emphasis | Useful contribution | Limitation if used alone |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Medical model | An individual's condition or impairment and clinical responses | Condition evidence, treatment and support needs | Can place responsibility for access entirely on the individual |
| Social model | Disabling barriers in environments, institutions and attitudes | Identifies changes the organisation can make | Does not replace a person's account of pain, fatigue or clinical needs |
| Interactional perspective | Functioning and participation emerge through person–environment interaction | Connects functional needs, task demands, context and support | Needs specific observations to become a useful design method |

WHO describes disability in terms of interaction between health conditions and personal and environmental factors [S01]. Scope explains the social model and its focus on barriers [S02]. These perspectives inform this repository; they are not interchangeable prevalence measures.

## Working vocabulary

| Term | Meaning here | Service example | Estimation caution |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Impairment or functional difference | A difference or limitation in a body or mental function | Difficulty distinguishing some colours | Does not establish disability identity or a particular barrier |
| Disability | Use the source's stated definition for statistics; recognise lived identity and social barriers | An inaccessible service obstructs participation | Survey, identity and administrative definitions differ |
| Apparent | May be noticed by another person in a specified interaction | A mobility aid visible in a shop | Depends on context and observer; do not infer diagnosis |
| Non-apparent / hidden | Not readily noticeable in that context; hidden is common terminology, not an implication of concealment | Fatigue or hearing loss not noticed in an online journey | Not simply total disability minus an apparent estimate |
| Lifelong / long-term | Describes duration, not constant severity | Lifelong dyslexia; long-term mobility impairment | Match duration definitions and age bands |
| Temporary | A time-limited change in function | A wrist injury affecting input | Annual cases are not the number affected on one day |
| Fluctuating / episodic | Needs vary over time or recur | Variable fatigue or migraine-related access needs | A single observation can miss periods of difficulty |
| Age-related | A change associated with ageing, with substantial individual variation | Changing hearing or dexterity | Age is not a diagnosis or proof of incapacity |
| Situational | A task circumstance constrains interaction | Holding a child while using a phone | Count affected interactions and exposure, not a new permanent demographic |
| Environmental | Surroundings create or amplify a barrier | Glare, noise, crowding or inaccessible transport | Can affect disabled and non-disabled people differently |
| Emotional or cognitive state | Current circumstances affecting the demands someone can manage | Grief during a notification process | Do not diagnose from behaviour or treat distress as universal incapacity |
| Access need | What enables participation in a particular activity | Text alongside audio; more time; accessible authentication | Ask about the task and support, rather than requiring a diagnosis |
| Preference | A person's chosen way of interacting | Speech, text, larger type or a human channel | May be essential in one situation and optional in another |

Situational and environmental barriers overlap. W3C discusses accessibility benefits in circumstances such as bright sunlight or inability to listen to audio [S03]. This does not equate an everyday inconvenience with the lived experience of long-term disability.

## Functional dimensions to investigate

Vision and colour perception; hearing; speech and communication; mobility; reach, dexterity and motor control; attention and executive function; reading and language; memory and comprehension; stamina and fatigue; sensory processing; emotional demands. These are practical research dimensions, not a diagnostic taxonomy.

Do not force Deaf identity, neurodivergence or other personal identities into terminology participants reject. Use participants' preferred language and preserve source wording when interpreting statistics. Need, identity, diagnosis and administrative registration are separate fields.

## Example

A customer with dyslexia reads a pension explanation on a dimmed phone after a disrupted night's sleep. The service requires remembering figures across screens. Record the reading need, current circumstances, display context and memory demand separately. Consider comparable information on one screen, clear explanations, save-and-return and optional support. Do not label the customer incapable or assume a preferred channel.

## Human and agent use

People: use this vocabulary to ask better questions, not assign identities. Agents: describe observed barriers and requested support; do not infer disability, neurotype, intoxication, grief or capacity from clicks, typing, device settings or appearance.

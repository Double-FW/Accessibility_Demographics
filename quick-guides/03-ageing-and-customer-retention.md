# Age, changing needs and long-term customer retention

**A customer who can use your service today may need a different way to use it later.**

Customer experience (CX) includes the whole relationship with an organisation. User experience (UX) concerns the experience of using a product or service. Both matter when someone stays with a bank, insurer, pension provider or subscription service for years.

## What is a prevalence curve?

Prevalence means the proportion of a defined population with a condition or functional difficulty at a particular time. An age-prevalence curve shows how that proportion differs by age. It is not a prediction of what will happen to a particular person.

Different needs have different patterns. A single “disability curve” hides important differences.

| Need | What the evidence helps us see | Implication for the experience |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Hearing | Hearing loss becomes more common in older age groups; RNID estimates about 55% among over-50s and almost 80% at 70+ | Keep written information, captions and support alternatives usable |
| Sight | Sight loss is concentrated in older populations; RNIB estimates around one in five people aged 75+ have sight loss | Support readable content, resizing, contrast and nonvisual access |
| Movement and dexterity | Calculations using the UK Family Resources Survey suggest about 9.2% of working-age people are disabled and report mobility impairment, versus 30.6% at pension age | Investigate reach, input precision, authentication and accessible support |
| Memory and thinking | Some acquired conditions become more common with age; cognitive access needs also occur at younger ages | Reduce unnecessary memory demands and make tasks resumable |
| Lifelong differences | A difference may be lifelong even when recognition or diagnosis happens later | Keep established preferences and access choices through redesigns |

These are different measures, not points on one comparable graph. Mobility is not a direct dexterity measure. The hearing thresholds overlap: over-50s already include over-70s. Use age bands that do not overlap and consistent definitions before drawing a numerical curve. Where data are missing, show the gap rather than invent a smooth line.

## Plan for the relationship

1. Map how customers use the service over five, ten or more years, using a horizon relevant to the product.
2. Identify important moments: renewal, changed circumstances, a major decision, recovery from difficulty and chosen support.
3. Check that authentication, documents and human support remain usable as well as the main interface.
4. Include older people with varied experience and younger people with similar functional needs in research.
5. Track understanding, effort, continued use and customer choice alongside retention.

**Example:** A pension customer may return after a long absence. A clear summary, saved progress and an understandable next step can help them resume. A redesign that removes familiar routes can make the relationship harder to maintain even when the underlying product has not changed.

Population ageing does not automatically predict your customer base: acquisition, exits and product eligibility also matter. Nor does age establish incapacity or a preferred channel. Ask customers and preserve autonomy.

# Environmental and situational barriers: planning for real life

**Design for the circumstances in which customers actually use the service.**

An environmental barrier comes from surroundings: glare, background noise, poor connectivity or a space that is difficult to navigate. A situational barrier comes from the activity or circumstances: holding a child, carrying bags, being interrupted or needing privacy.

These descriptions overlap. People sometimes call them environmental or situational disabilities. For planning, it is usually clearer to describe the constraint and the barrier it creates. A noisy carriage does not give someone a hearing impairment, but it can make an audio-only message unusable. Disabled customers can encounter these barriers alongside long-term needs.

## Start with situations, then look at the task

| Situation | Possible barrier | Candidate improvement to test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sunlight or a dimmed screen | Important information is hard to distinguish | Readable text, strong contrast and cues beyond colour |
| Muted audio or a noisy place | Spoken content or alerts are missed | Text alternatives, captions and appropriate visible feedback |
| One hand occupied, left or right | Controls are hard to reach or manipulate | Reachable actions and alternatives to complex gestures |
| An interrupted journey | Customers lose context or entered information | Saved progress, clear status and a useful return point |
| A shared space | Speech input or read-aloud output exposes private information | Private text-based options and customer-controlled modes |

These are hypotheses to investigate, not claims that each change solves every situation. Additional simultaneous alerts, for example, may create distraction for another customer.

## How to plan for them

1. Choose an important journey and ask when, where and why people use it.
2. Include these questions in ordinary customer interviews and accessible diaries.
3. Test realistic, safe conditions alongside different access needs and preferences.
4. Offer flexible ways to receive information, act, pause, correct and get help.
5. Measure whether the task remains understandable and usable, not just whether a control can technically be activated.

Build recurring improvements into components, content templates, branding guidance and support processes. A captioned advertisement still needs a usable route to the offer and purchase.

## Work out how often it matters

Count relevant journeys, not a new permanent population. If a suitable local study finds that 20% of a journey's attempts happen one-handed, that is an exposure estimate for that task and period. It does not mean 20% of all customers have a disability or that every one-handed attempt fails.

Time of day, fatigue and illness can add demands, but people vary. Do not assume a universal morning or evening capability curve. Driving also needs a task-suitability decision: voice alone does not make a distracting task appropriate; postponing it may be the right design outcome.

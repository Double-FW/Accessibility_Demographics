# Medical and social models: a marketing and product guide

**The useful question is both “Who might need this?” and “What is stopping them using it?”**

A disability model is a way of explaining disability. It is not a customer segment. The medical model focuses on an individual's impairment or condition and clinical responses. The social model focuses on barriers in society, organisations and environments. Scope explains how removing those barriers supports choice and independence.

Using medical information does not automatically mean adopting a medical model. A team can use hearing-loss evidence to understand its audience while taking responsibility for changing an inaccessible service.

## What each perspective helps you investigate

| Perspective | Useful question | What it cannot tell you alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Condition and functional evidence | How many intended customers may have relevant needs? | Whether this interface works, what someone prefers, or why they leave |
| Social model | Which design choices, policies or attitudes obstruct participation? | A condition's prevalence or every individual's experience |
| Person in context | How do needs, surroundings, technology and the task combine? | A universal solution without customer research |

## What this means for marketing

Ask whether people can recognise the brand, understand the offer and respond through a usable route. A campaign featuring disabled people can still exclude customers if the offer is presented only through inaccessible media or the response route requires a phone call.

Use demographic evidence to plan who to include in research. Then test the actual message, format, offer and route to purchase. Disability is one aspect of a person's experience; their views about price, trust and quality matter too.

## What this means for product

Start with a customer goal. Inspect the whole journey, including sign-in, payment, confirmation and support. Change the product or process where it creates an avoidable barrier. Ask about support and preferences without making a diagnosis the price of entry.

**Example:** A hearing-loss estimate suggests a significant audience for a service. A barrier review finds that customers must telephone to correct an order. An accessible written route, with equivalent response quality, is a candidate improvement. Research should establish whether it works for the intended customers; the population estimate does not prove its sales effect.

## Try this with one journey

1. Describe the customer goal in one sentence.
2. Note relevant population evidence and its limits.
3. List the demands the service makes: seeing, hearing, speaking, remembering, reaching and timing.
4. Ask customers what obstructs them and what would help.
5. Compare understanding, effort, successful use and confidence after a change.

Do not turn “disabled customers” into one persona. People with the same condition can need different options; people with different circumstances can encounter the same barrier.

# Neurodivergence and better usability research

**Including neurodivergent people helps teams discover experiences a narrow research sample can miss.**

Neurodivergence is an umbrella term used for differences in how people think, learn, process information and experience the world. In these guides it includes autism, ADHD, dyslexia and developmental coordination disorder/dyspraxia. The term is not one diagnosis or one set of needs. People may identify differently and can have overlapping conditions.

Neurodiversity describes variation across people. Neurodivergent describes a person or people whose neurological experience differs from what is treated as typical. Neither tells you someone's intelligence, preferences or ability to make decisions. Colour vision deficiency is considered separately here as a perceptual difference.

## What can research help you investigate?

| Demand in a service | Questions to explore with customers |
| --- | --- |
| Reading and interpreting information | Is the language clear, the layout helpful and the important distinction understandable? |
| Attention and interruption | Can someone resume without remembering everything or starting again? |
| Memory and sequencing | Are options, progress and consequences available when needed? |
| Predictability and sensory demands | Can people anticipate what happens, control competing content and recover from surprises? |
| Movement and input | Can people act accurately without unnecessary precision or complicated gestures? |

These are task questions, not a diagnostic checklist. No participant represents everyone with a shared label. People also bring strengths, expertise and established strategies that research should respect.

## Why it is worthwhile

Clearer choices, useful feedback, saved progress and recoverable errors are promising improvements for many customers. Research with neurodivergent people can reveal where a journey creates avoidable work and help teams explore alternatives before committing to a design.

The benefit is the opportunity to make better-informed decisions. Inclusion alone does not guarantee an improvement, and a change preferred by one participant can create difficulty for another. Test the changes across the relevant audience. Keep different preferences visible and offer control where appropriate.

## Make inclusion part of ordinary research

1. Include neurodivergent people in concept, message, price, brand and usability research, not only specialist accessibility sessions.
2. Recruit a useful range of experiences over the research programme. Small qualitative samples are for understanding problems, not estimating prevalence.
3. Ask about participation arrangements, preferred communication, pacing and breaks. Avoid unnecessary diagnostic proof.
4. Test complete meaningful tasks using participants' preferred tools where appropriate. Observe understanding, effort, errors, recovery and confidence.
5. Discuss interpretations with participants and record which decisions changed. Check improvements with other affected customers too.

**Example:** A subscription cancellation flow loses its state after sign-in. Research reveals confusion about whether cancellation happened. A saved state, clear confirmation and a recoverable next step are candidate improvements. Their usefulness depends on the implementation and actual evaluation, not on a claim that every neurodivergent person responds alike.

Do not assume a specialist font, simplified screen or distraction-free mode is universally suitable. Ask what helps the actual task and maintain meaningful choice.

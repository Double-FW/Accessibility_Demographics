# Understanding audiences through functional needs: the EAA approach

**Ask what someone needs to do, rather than requiring them to name a condition.**

The European Accessibility Act (EAA) includes functional performance criteria in **Annex I, Section VII**. These describe ways products and services need to work. They can also help marketing, product and research teams think about people their usual audience descriptions may miss.

They are not population statistics or mutually exclusive demographic groups. The Act has specific conditions for their legal use; using them here as a research lens is a practical adaptation, not a substitute for the other requirements.

## Eleven useful lenses

The following are plain-language summaries, with the original paragraph letters for reference.

| Paragraph | Ask whether the experience supports… |
| --- | --- |
| (a) | Someone who cannot rely on sight |
| (b) | Someone with reduced sight |
| (c) | Understanding without distinguishing colours |
| (d) | Access when sound cannot be heard |
| (e) | Access with reduced hearing |
| (f) | Input without speaking or other vocal sounds |
| (g) | Control with reduced dexterity or force |
| (h) | Control within a shorter reach |
| (i) | Protection against flashing that can provoke seizures |
| (j) | Support when thinking and understanding demands are difficult |
| (k) | Private use of accessibility features |

Privacy applies across the experience. It is not a separate demographic to count. A person may need several of the other forms of support at once.

## Turn the lenses into customer questions

For a comparison-and-purchase journey, ask:

- Can people understand the differences without colour alone?
- Can they get help without a spoken conversation?
- Can they compare options without remembering information from another screen?
- Can they use their chosen access method without revealing private information?

Ask about the activity and available options: “Would written information, a conversation, or both help you compare these choices?” Allow multiple needs and an option not to answer. A preference is not evidence of a diagnosis.

## Use them to plan research

1. Choose a real task and identify the functional demands at each step.
2. Invite people with relevant lived experience, including combinations of needs.
3. Offer accessible participation and preferred technology or communication.
4. Observe understanding, accurate completion, effort, confidence and control.
5. Identify a change and check it across other affected customers too.

Use population research separately when estimating scale. The percentage of people reporting hearing loss is not the percentage who prefer sign language, and neither directly measures the number affected by a particular audio-only step. Avoid adding overlapping need groups.

**Example:** For a bank comparison tool, colour-independent information, clear explanations and optional written support can address several needs through one shared journey. Research determines the actual benefit.
